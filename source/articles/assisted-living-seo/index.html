Assisted Living SEO: A Practical Checklist for Independent Homes

Assisted living SEO is the work of making sure a family who searches for care in your town finds your home, understands what you offer and calls. For a small independent home, start with your Google Business Profile and your reviews. Then build a clear page for each type of care you provide, a page about cost and a page for each nearby town you really serve. Claim and correct your directory listings. Judge the work by calls, tours and move-ins, not by rankings alone.

We own and run an assisted living and memory care home ourselves. This checklist is how we think about search for a small home, not a report of client results. It is part of our larger senior living marketing guide.

How families actually search

Start with the person on the other end of the search. Most of the time it is not the future resident. It is an adult daughter or son, often after a fall or a hospital stay, being told that Mom cannot go home alone. They have a few days to find a place. They are on a phone, sometimes in a hospital hallway.

They search the way people talk:

  • "assisted living in" plus the name of your town
  • "memory care near me"
  • "respite care for dementia" plus a nearby town
  • "how much does assisted living cost"
  • Your home's name, once someone has mentioned it

Every item on this checklist exists to answer one of those searches clearly and quickly, on a small screen, for someone who is tired and worried.

1. Get your Google Business Profile right first

For a search with a town name or "near me" in it, the map results and the Business Profile are often the first thing a family sees. It is free, and it is the most important page you do not host yourself.

  • Primary category. Pick the one that matches your main service, such as assisted living facility. Add other categories only for care you actually provide.
  • Name. Use your real business name, the one on your sign and your license. Do not add keywords to it.
  • Hours. Show the hours someone can call or visit, and keep holiday hours current.
  • Phone. A number a person answers. If the main line goes to a phone tree, families will hang up and call the next home.
  • Photos. The real building, the front door, a real room, the dining room, the courtyard. Families want to know what they will walk into.
  • Services. List assisted living, memory care, respite stays and anything else you really offer, in plain words.
  • Website link. Point it to your own website, not a directory page.

2. Ask for reviews the same way, every time

Reviews matter more here than in most businesses, because families are trusting you with a parent. The homes with steady reviews are usually the ones that ask in a steady way.

  • Pick one moment to ask, such as the 30-day care meeting, when a family has seen how things really work.
  • Ask in person, then send a short text or email with the direct review link.
  • Ask every family, not only the ones you expect to be happy.
  • Never offer anything in return. Google's review policy prohibits offering incentives in exchange for reviews, and that includes gift cards, discounts and small thank-you gifts.

Replying to reviews without breaking privacy

Reply to every review, good or bad, and keep it short. The rule we follow: never confirm in a reply that someone is or was a resident, and never mention any health detail, even if the reviewer did. Thank them, invite them to call the administrator directly, and give the number. A reply like "Thank you for taking the time to write. We would welcome a call at our main number so we can talk with you directly" works for almost any review. Privacy rules for care homes can vary, so check your state's rules and your own policies.

3. Give each type of care its own page

A single "Services" page that lists everything in one paragraph does not answer anyone's question well. A family looking for memory care wants a page about memory care. Build one page for each kind of care you really provide:

  • Assisted living. What help is included, what a day looks like, what a room is like.
  • Memory care. How the space is secured, how staff are trained, what activities look like, how you handle hard days. According to the AHCA/NCAL Facts and Figures page, read in November 2026, about 4 in 10 assisted living residents have Alzheimer's or another dementia, so many families who call about assisted living are really asking about memory needs too.
  • Respite or short stays. Whether you offer them, the shortest stay you accept and how to book one.

Each page should answer the main question in its first two sentences, name your town, and end with your phone number and a way to request a tour. How these pages fit into the site as a whole is in senior living website design.

4. Add a page for each nearby town you really serve

Families often search from the town where they live, not the town where your home is. If you regularly take residents from three or four nearby towns, give each one a page.

Make those pages useful, not copies. Say how far you are and how long the drive is, which hospitals and clinics nearby families tend to use, and whether you have residents who came from that town. Do not build pages for towns you do not really serve. A page that only swaps one town name for another helps no one and can look like spam.

5. Publish a page about what it costs

Cost is one of the first things families search. If your site does not mention it, they will find the answer somewhere else, usually on a directory page that does not know your rates.

You do not have to publish a full rate sheet. At a minimum, give a starting monthly price, what it includes, and what changes it: room type, level of care, memory care, medication help. For context, the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey, published in March 2026, puts the national median cost of assisted living at $6,200 a month, or $74,400 a year. Your local costs may be quite different, so explain yours plainly. Say which payment options you accept and which you do not.

6. Claim your directory listings, then build your own site

For broad searches like "assisted living near me," large senior living directories and referral services, such as A Place for Mom, Caring.com and Seniorly, usually rank above a small home's own website. They have years of content and thousands of listings. A small home will rarely outrank them for those broad searches, and it does not need to.

Do two things instead:

  1. Claim and correct those listings. Make sure your name, address, phone, photos, care types and description are right. Families read them, and a wrong phone number on a directory is a lost call.
  2. Build your own site to win the searches you can win. When someone hears your name from a discharge planner, a neighbor or a directory, they search it. Your own site and Business Profile should be what they find first, with your number on it.

Before you sign any agreement with a referral service, read how it gets paid. Our assisted living marketing ideas article covers how those fees commonly work.

7. Keep your name, address and phone the same everywhere

Write down one reference version of your business name, street address and main phone number. Then check that it matches on your website, your Business Profile, Bing Places, Apple Maps, Facebook, your state licensing listing if your state has a public one, and every directory you are on. Fix wrong phone numbers and old addresses first. Keep a simple list of what you checked and when.

8. Make the site fast on a phone

A family member searching from a hospital will not wait for a slow page. Open your own site on your phone, on cellular data, not office wifi. If the main content takes more than a few seconds to show up, fix it. The usual causes are huge photos, sliders, video that plays on load and pop-ups. Make the phone number tap-to-call on every page.

9. Write clear facts that AI answers can use

More families now ask ChatGPT or see a Google AI answer before they click anything. These tools pull from pages that state facts plainly. Help them by putting the basics in plain sentences on your own site:

  • Your name, town and the types of care you provide
  • How many residents you serve and whether you have memory care
  • Your starting price and what it includes
  • Whether you offer respite stays
  • How to schedule a tour

There is no special code that gets you into AI answers. Clear, accurate pages are the best you can do. Ask an AI tool about assisted living in your town every few months and check whether what it says about you is right.

What to measure

Rankings are easy to look at and easy to fool yourself with. What matters is whether families call, tour and move in. Track these by source each month:

MeasureHow to count it
CallsReal inquiries about care, not vendors, job seekers or current families
Web inquiriesTour requests and contact forms from real families
ToursTours that actually happened, in person or by video
Move-insResidents who moved in, with where the family first heard of you

Ask every family on the first call how they found you, and write it down. Use a separate phone number on your website if you can, so you can tell website calls from Business Profile calls.

How long this takes

Expect months, not weeks. Fixing your Business Profile and listings can help sooner. New pages and a steady flow of reviews take longer to show up in search. Nobody can promise you a position in Google, and you should be wary of anyone who does. Do the work, check that it is live, and judge it by calls, tours and move-ins over a full season.

FAQ

What is assisted living SEO?

It is the work of helping families who search for care in your area find your home on Google, maps and AI answers, and then call or ask for a tour. For a small home that means a complete Business Profile, steady reviews, a clear page for each type of care, a cost page and correct directory listings.

How long does SEO take for an assisted living home?

Expect months, not weeks. Fixing your Business Profile and listings can help sooner, while new pages and reviews take longer. No one can promise a ranking. Judge the work by calls, tours and move-ins over a full season.

Should an assisted living home list its prices online?

We think so. Families search for cost early, and a page with a starting monthly price, what it includes and what changes it builds trust. If you do not publish anything, families will look for the answer on other sites that may not know your rates.

Do we need our own website if we are listed on senior living directories?

Yes. Directories usually rank above small homes for broad searches, so claim and correct those listings. But families who hear your name will search for it, and your own website and Business Profile should be what they find, with your own phone number.

How should we reply to reviews without breaking privacy?

Keep replies short and general. Never confirm that someone is or was a resident and never mention health details, even if the reviewer did. Thank them and invite them to call the administrator directly. Check your state's rules and your own privacy policies.
