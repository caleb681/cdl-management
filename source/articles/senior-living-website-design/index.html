Senior Living Website Design: What an Assisted Living Website Needs

A good assisted living website answers five questions on the first screen of a phone: what care you provide, where you are, what it costs, whether a family can tour and who to call. Behind that, it needs a page for each type of care, a clear cost range, real photos of your real building, a short tour form and a phone number someone answers. It should be easy to read, fast on a phone and owned by you.

Who is really on your website

Picture the person before you pick a design. Most visitors to a senior living website are adult children, not the future resident. Often a parent has just had a fall or a hospital stay. The visitor is on a phone, stressed, short on time and comparing three to five homes, sometimes in one evening.

That person is not looking for a mood. They want to know quickly whether you can care for their parent, what it will cost, and whether they can trust you. Every design choice should make those answers easier to find.

Answer five questions on the first screen

On a phone, before anyone scrolls, the top of your home page should answer:

  1. What care do you provide? Assisted living, memory care, respite stays. Say it in words, not just a tagline.
  2. Where are you? Your town, and the nearby towns you serve.
  3. What does it cost? A starting price, or a link straight to your cost page.
  4. Can I tour? One clear button to request a tour.
  5. Who do I call? A tap-to-call phone number that a person answers.

A large photo with a line like "Where every day feels like home" answers none of these. Keep the warmth, but put the facts first.

Pages every assisted living website needs

  • Care levels. What assisted living includes at your home, and how you decide what level of help a resident needs.
  • Memory care. If you offer it, its own page: the space, staff training, daily routine and how families stay involved.
  • Pricing. A starting price or a clear range, and what changes it.
  • A day in the life. What a real weekday looks like, hour by hour, in plain words.
  • Meals and activities. A sample menu and a real activity calendar.
  • Staff. Who runs the home, who the nurse or wellness lead is, and how long key staff have been there, if they agree to be named.
  • Tour and contact. Your address, map, phone, hours and a short tour form.
  • FAQ. The questions families ask on the first call, answered honestly.
  • For families. Resources for the person making the decision: what to bring on move-in day, how to talk with a parent about moving, local support groups.

Publish your pricing, or at least where it starts

Many homes hide price because it depends on care needs. We understand the worry, but hiding it costs trust. A family that cannot find any number will often assume the worst, or call the next home that did post one.

You can be honest without a full rate sheet:

  • Give a starting monthly price for assisted living, and for memory care if you offer it.
  • Say what is included in that price.
  • List what changes it, such as room size, level of care and medication help.
  • Say which payment options you accept.

For context, the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey, published in March 2026, puts the national median for assisted living at $6,200 a month. Your local costs may be different, and a family will trust your own number more than a national one.

Show the real building and the real rooms

Families will visit. If the website shows a grand lobby and they walk into a small, homey house, they feel misled, even if the house is lovely. Use real photos of your real building: the front entrance, a resident room as it actually looks, the dining room at mealtime, the outdoor space, the memory care area. Take them on a bright day with the rooms tidy. A phone camera in good light is better than a stock photo.

Many families also value a short video walk-through. It does not need to be polished. It needs to be true.

Resident photos and stories need written consent

Photos of residents enjoying a day can say more than any paragraph. They also carry real privacy duties. Our rule: no resident's face, name or story goes on the website or social media without written consent on file. Where a resident cannot give consent, get it in writing from their legal representative. Keep the signed forms, and take a photo down the same day if a family asks. Staff photos need permission too. Consent and privacy rules vary, so check your state's rules and talk with your own advisor if you are unsure.

Make it easy to call, tour and hear back

  • Tap-to-call on every page, in the same place, on a phone.
  • A short tour form. Name, phone, email, preferred day and an optional note. Do not ask for medical history on a web form.
  • A fast reply. Answer web requests within the hour when you are open, and first thing the next morning when you are not. A form that sends to an inbox nobody checks is worse than no form.
  • Tell people what happens next. "We will call you today to set a time" is better than "Thank you for your submission."

Make it easy to read for everyone

Some of your visitors are older adults themselves, and some are reading through tears. Design for that.

  • Large body text, and a lot of space between lines.
  • Dark text on a light background with strong contrast. No light gray on white.
  • Buttons big enough to tap without zooming.
  • Describe each photo with alt text for people using screen readers.
  • Plain words. Say "help with bathing and dressing," not "ADL support."

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are a good standard to ask your designer to follow.

Make it fast on a phone

A family member in a hospital waiting room is on a weak signal. Test your own site on your phone, on cellular data. If the main content takes more than a few seconds to appear, it is too slow. The usual causes are oversized photos, background video, sliders and too many add-on scripts.

What to leave off

  • Stock photos of models. Families can tell, and it makes them wonder what else is staged.
  • "Luxury resort" language a small home cannot back up. Warm, honest and specific beats grand.
  • Medical claims and outcome promises. Do not promise that residents will improve, stay healthier or avoid hospital stays. Describe what you do, not results you cannot promise.
  • Pop-ups that cover the phone number on a small screen.
  • Chat widgets nobody answers. An unanswered chat tells a family that nobody is paying attention. If you cannot staff it, take it off.
  • Countdown offers and "only one room left" banners. This is a hard family decision. Pressure drives good families away.

What to ask a web designer before you hire one

  1. Who owns the website and the domain name? The domain should be registered in your business's name, on an account you can log in to.
  2. Can we leave, and take the site with us? Ask what you get if you stop paying: the content, the photos, the design files.
  3. Who makes updates, and how fast? Prices, menus, staff and photos change. Find out who changes them and how long it takes.
  4. Is it month to month or a contract? Ask how long the commitment is and what it costs to end early.
  5. Have you built sites for care homes before? Ask how they handle resident photos and consent.
  6. How will we know if it is working? The answer should be calls, tour requests and move-ins, not page views.

FAQ

What should an assisted living website include?

A first screen that says what care you provide, where you are, what it costs, how to tour and who to call. Then a page for each type of care, a pricing page, a day in the life, meals and activities, staff, tour and contact, an FAQ and resources for families.

Should an assisted living website show prices?

Yes, at least a starting price, what it includes and what changes it. Families compare several homes, and a home that hides cost often loses trust. A clear range is better than no number at all.

Can we use photos of residents on our website?

Only with written consent on file. Where a resident cannot consent, get written consent from their legal representative. Remove a photo promptly if a family asks. Rules vary, so check your state's rules.

Should we use stock photos on a senior living website?

Avoid them where you can. Families will visit, and stock photos of models make your home look different from what they will see. Real photos of your real building and rooms, taken in good light, build more trust.

What should we ask a web designer before hiring?

Ask who owns the website and the domain, whether you can leave and take the site with you, who makes updates and how fast, whether it is month to month or a contract, and how you will measure calls, tours and move-ins.
