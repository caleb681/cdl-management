Senior living marketing: a practical guide for independent homes

Practical guidance from the CDL Marketing team.

By Caleb Foglesong · Published November 18, 2026

The short version: senior living marketing for a small home is mostly four things done well. Be easy to find when a family searches your town. Answer every call and web inquiry fast. Stay in front of the people who refer families. Know where each move-in came from and what it cost. Advertising comes after those, not before.

This guide is for owners and administrators of independent assisted living and memory care homes. It is not written for a chain with a regional marketing team. We own and run an assisted living and memory care home ourselves, so this is the order we would work in. If you would rather have it done for you, see our assisted living marketing service.

Who is really making the decision

The person searching is rarely the future resident. It is usually an adult daughter or son, often on a phone, often after something has gone wrong: a fall, a hospital stay, a diagnosis, or a parent who can no longer be left alone. They are tired and worried, and many of them have never looked for care before.

That changes what good marketing looks like. They are not shopping for fun. They want straight answers to a short list of questions:

  • Can you care for someone with my mom's needs?
  • Where are you, and how far is that from me?
  • What does it cost, and what makes the price go up?
  • Is there a room now?
  • Can I come see it, and who do I talk to?

A home that answers those five questions clearly, on its website and on the phone, is ahead of most of the homes around it. Pressure does not work here and it should not be tried. No countdowns, no "only one room left" unless that is simply true, no fear. Helpful and honest wins.

Where move-ins come from

Most small homes fill rooms from four sources. Each one costs something different.

SourceWhat it costsWhat it takes
Professional referrals (discharge planners, home health, hospice, elder law attorneys, physicians)Your timeRegular visits and being easy to reach when they call
Word of mouth from familiesNothing extraGood care and good communication with families
Families who find you online and contact you directlyA website, a Google listing and steady upkeepBeing findable for your town and answering fast
Paid referral directoriesA fee per move-in, set by your contractSpeed, because the same family is often sent to several homes

Referral directories are a real source for many homes, and there is nothing wrong with using them. They are commonly paid by the community when a referred family moves in, and the fee is commonly tied to roughly the first month of rent and care. Terms vary, so read your own agreement. The point for your marketing is simple: every move-in you earn directly is one you do not pay that fee on.

For scale, the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey, published in March 2026, put the national median cost of assisted living at $6,200 a month. Your rate will differ. Use your own number when you do the math later in this guide.

Fix these first

Do these before spending on ads. Most of them are free.

1. Answer the phone and reply the same hour

A family that calls three homes usually tours the ones that answered. Find out what happens to a call at 6 pm on a Saturday. Find out who gets the website form and how long a reply takes. If the honest answer is "it depends who is working," fix that first. Nothing else in this guide pays off if inquiries go unanswered.

2. Your Google Business Profile

This is the listing that shows on Google Maps and beside search results. Check the name, address, phone, hours and category. Add real photos of the building, a room, the dining area and the grounds. Describe the care you provide in plain words. For many small homes this listing gets more views than the website does.

3. A website that answers the five questions

Care levels, location, cost, how to tour and a phone number that can be tapped. Real photos, not stock models. It has to load quickly and read well on a phone. We go through it page by page in what an assisted living website needs.

4. Reviews

Ask every family, the same way, at a calm moment. Never offer anything in return, because Google prohibits incentives for reviews. When you reply to a review, thank the person and keep it general. Never confirm that someone is or was a resident, and never mention anything about health or care.

5. Your listings on other sites

Search your home's name. Check what the large directories and state listings say about you. Wrong phone numbers, old photos and out-of-date care types cost you families who never tell you why they did not call.

Then build a referral rhythm

Professional referrals are earned in person. Make a short list of the people in your area who talk with families at the moment of decision: hospital and rehab discharge planners, home health and hospice agencies, elder law attorneys, physicians' offices, senior centers and churches.

Visit on a schedule you can keep, even if it is two stops a week. Bring something useful: a one-page sheet with the care you provide, what you cannot provide, your price range and whether you have a room today. Referrers remember the home that is honest about what it cannot take. Check your state's rules on gifts and payments to referral sources before you bring anything of value.

What to say

A small independent home cannot outspend a chain and should not try to sound like one. Say what is true and specific about your home.

  • Small is the point. Staff who know every resident by name is something a family can feel on a tour. Say it plainly.
  • Be clear about cost. Publish your prices, or at least a starting price and what changes it. Families trust the home that tells them first.
  • Show real daily life. Meals, activities, the garden, long-tenured staff. Use resident photos and stories only with written consent on file, and from the legal representative when the resident cannot give it.
  • Do not promise outcomes. No medical claims, no "she will thrive here." Describe what you do, not what will happen.

When to add paid advertising

Add it when the basics are in place and rooms are still open. Start with Google search ads, because they reach a family at the moment they are looking for assisted living in your town. Send the click to a page that answers the five questions and has a phone number and a short tour form.

Know the rules before you build anything. Google does not let housing ads in the United States and Canada target people by age, gender, parental status, marital status or ZIP code. Radius and city targeting are allowed. Google also treats health as a sensitive category, which limits the use of your own contact lists for ads. Meta, which owns Facebook and Instagram, has a Housing special ad category with similar limits. Assume these rules apply to a senior living home, and check each platform's current policy before you launch.

In practice that means your ads are built around what people search and how close they are to you, not around who they are. That suits this decision anyway.

How to judge it

Rankings and clicks are not the goal. Move-ins are. Keep one simple sheet and fill it in for every inquiry:

  • The date, and how they found you. Ask every time.
  • Whether they toured.
  • Whether they moved in.

At the end of each quarter, total it by source. Then work out cost per move-in for each: what you spent on that source, divided by the move-ins it produced. Include referral fees. A source that looks cheap per inquiry can be expensive per move-in, and the other way around.

Give it time. A family may take weeks or months between the first call and move-in day, so one month of numbers will mislead you. A quarter is the shortest fair test.

A sensible order for the next 90 days

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: test your own phone and web form. Fix who answers and how fast. Start the inquiry sheet.
  2. Weeks 3 to 6: correct the Google Business Profile and the big directory listings. Add real photos. Start asking every family for a review.
  3. Weeks 7 to 10: fix the website: care levels, cost, tour, phone. Set the referral visit schedule.
  4. Weeks 11 to 13: read the sheet. If rooms are still open, test Google search ads with a small budget.

Common questions

What is senior living marketing?

It is everything that helps a family find your home, trust it and book a tour: your Google listing, your website, your reviews, your relationships with the people who refer families, and any advertising you pay for. For a small home, most of it is getting the basics right and answering quickly.

Where do most assisted living move-ins come from?

Usually four places: professional referrals such as hospital discharge planners and home health agencies, word of mouth from families, families who search online and contact you directly, and paid referral directories. The mix is different for every home, so write down the source of every inquiry and every move-in.

How much should an assisted living home spend on marketing?

There is no single right number. Work backward from what one move-in is worth to your home and what you already pay per move-in through referral fees. Fix the free basics first, then test one paid channel with a small budget and judge it by cost per move-in.

Do Google and Facebook limit ads for senior living?

Yes, in some ways. Google does not let housing ads in the United States and Canada target by age, gender, parental status, marital status or ZIP code, and it treats health as a sensitive category for audience lists. Meta has a Housing special ad category with similar limits. Senior living advertisers should assume these rules apply and plan ads around search terms and a radius around the home.

How long does senior living marketing take to work?

Answering inquiries faster helps the same week. A corrected Google listing and new reviews usually show within a few weeks. Ranking your own website for searches in your town takes months, and nobody can promise a position. Families also take time to decide, so judge the work over a quarter, not a week.
