Assisted Living Marketing Ideas, and a Simple Plan to Use Them

Practical guidance from the CDL Marketing team.

By Caleb Foglesong · Published November 18, 2026

The best assisted living marketing ideas for a small home are plain ones: be easy to find, be the home local professionals trust enough to refer, be known in your town, answer every inquiry fast and give families a reason to talk about you. Pick a few, do them every month, and judge them by inquiries, tours and move-ins by source. Below are 15 ideas and a simple 90-day plan to use them.

We own and run an assisted living and memory care home ourselves. These are ideas we would use in a small home, not a report of client results. For the bigger picture, see our senior living marketing guide. If you would rather have this done for you, see our assisted living marketing service.

Be found

1. Complete your Google Business Profile

Right primary category, real hours, a phone number a person answers, real photos of your building and every type of care you provide listed as a service. For many families searching your town, this is the first thing they see.

2. Ask every family for a review, the same way

Pick one moment, such as the 30-day care meeting, and ask every family at that point with a direct link. Never offer anything in return. Google's review policy prohibits offering incentives in exchange for reviews. When you reply, never confirm that someone is or was a resident and never mention health details.

3. Fix the website basics

Tap-to-call on every page, a starting price, a page for each type of care, real photos and a short tour form. Our senior living website design article goes page by page.

4. Claim and correct your directory listings

Large senior living directories and referral services often show up above small homes in search. Families read them, so make sure your name, phone, photos and care types are right on each one. The search side is in our assisted living SEO checklist.

Be referred

For many small homes, the best move-ins come from people who already know a family needs help. These relationships are built in person, slowly, by being useful.

5. Hospital discharge planners and case managers

They are trying to find a safe place for a patient, often within days. Make their job easier: a one-page sheet with your care types, whether you have a room open, starting price, what you can and cannot handle, and a direct number that gets an answer. Update them when a room opens.

6. Home health and hospice agencies

Their nurses are in family homes every day and see when someone can no longer live alone. Get to know the intake staff and the field nurses. If you work with hospice in your home, make sure they know how that goes.

7. Elder law attorneys and financial planners

Families ask them how to pay for care. They want to know what you cost and which payment options you accept. Give them a straight answer and your cost page.

8. Churches and faith communities

Pastors and parish nurses hear from families early. Offer to speak, host, or simply drop off information and introduce yourself.

9. Physicians and clinic staff

Family doctors and geriatric clinics are often the first to say "it may be time." The front desk and nurses are the people to know.

A simple monthly visit rhythm

You do not need a sales team. You need a list and a calendar.

  • Write down the 10 to 15 referral contacts that matter most within your area.
  • Visit or call each one once a month. Two or three visits a week covers the list.
  • Bring something useful: an updated room availability note, your one-page sheet, an invitation to an education night.
  • Write down every referral and where it came from.
  • Thank every referrer, without sharing private details about the family.

Be known locally

10. Host a caregiver support group

Offer your space once a month for a family caregiver or dementia caregiver support group, led by a trained facilitator or a local partner. It helps people who need it, and it brings families through your door with no pitch at all.

11. Hold family education nights

Short evening talks on questions families really have: how to pay for care, how to know when it is time, what memory care actually is. Invite an elder law attorney or a hospice nurse to speak. Keep it informational, not a sales event.

12. Hold an open house

Once or twice a year, open the doors with simple food, short tours and staff on hand to answer questions. Invite your referral contacts and neighbors, not just prospects.

13. Show real daily life on a local Facebook page

Post what a normal week looks like: the meal, the music, the birthday, the garden. Show residents only with written consent on file, and from the legal representative where a resident cannot consent. A steady, honest page tells families more than any ad.

Convert the inquiries you already get

14. Answer the phone, reply fast and follow up

This is where many homes lose families they already paid to reach.

  • Answer the phone. A person, not a phone tree, during the day. Check what callers hear after hours, and return every missed call the same day.
  • Reply to web inquiries within the same hour when you are open, and first thing the next morning when you are not.
  • Offer a tour within a few days. In person or by video if the family lives far away.
  • Follow up kindly. A call a couple of days after the tour, and a note after that. Answer their questions. Do not push. This is a hard decision, and families remember who was patient with them.

Keep families, and they will talk

15. Communicate well with current families

Word of mouth starts with the families you already serve. Call with good news, not only problems. Send a short monthly update with photos, shared only with consent. Make it easy to reach the administrator. Families who feel informed tell their friends, and their friends call.

A simple 90-day plan

Do not try all 15 at once. Here is the order we would use.

MonthFocusDone when
Month 1Fix the basics: Business Profile, website basics, directory listings, phone answering, inquiry trackingProfile complete, listings corrected, tap-to-call and tour form tested, every inquiry logged with its source
Month 2Referral rhythm and reviews: build the referral list, start monthly visits, start asking every family for a review10 to 15 contacts visited, review requests sent to every family at the agreed moment
Month 3If rooms are still open, add one paid test, such as Google search ads, plus one local eventAds live with their own phone number, one support group, education night or open house held

Give each item an owner. In a small home that is often the administrator for referral visits, the business office for reviews and tracking, and the activity director for events and Facebook. Put a 30-minute check-in on the calendar once a month to look at the numbers together.

If every room is full by month 3, skip the paid test. Keep the referral visits and reviews going, and start a short waiting list so you can call families back when a room opens. If rooms are still open, start the ads small, in the towns you really serve, and give them a full two to three months before you judge them.

A note on ad rules before a paid test

Google's restricted targeting rules for personalized advertising, as of November 2026, say housing ads in the US and Canada cannot be targeted by age, gender, parental status, marital status or ZIP code. Radius and city targeting are allowed. Health is also a sensitive interest category, where lists an advertiser builds, such as customer match or remarketing lists, cannot be used. Senior living advertisers should assume these rules apply to them. Meta has a Housing special ad category with similar limits, so check whether your Facebook ads fall under it before you build an audience. A simple search campaign aimed at people typing "assisted living" and your town works within those limits.

How to judge it

Do not judge this by likes, page views or rankings. Judge it by what happens to families. Every month, count by source:

  • Inquiries. Real calls and forms from families looking for care.
  • Tours. Tours that actually happened.
  • Move-ins. Residents who moved in, and where the family first heard about you.
  • Cost per move-in. What each source cost you that month, in money and staff time, divided by the move-ins it produced.

Move-ins come slowly in a small home, so look at three to six months together before you decide something works or does not. Ask every family on the first call how they heard about you, and write it down.

Where paid referral services fit

Paid referral services are a real source of move-ins for many homes, and there is nothing wrong with using them. Families find them when they search, and the services help them compare options. Understand how they get paid. They commonly charge the community a fee when a family they referred moves in, often tied to roughly the first month's rent and care. Terms vary by contract, so read your own agreement, including how a referral is defined and how long it counts.

That is why the ideas above matter. Every move-in your home earns directly, from your own Business Profile, website, referral partners or a family's word of mouth, is one you do not pay that fee on.

FAQ

What are good marketing ideas for a small assisted living home?

Start with the basics: a complete Google Business Profile, steady reviews, a clear website and correct directory listings. Then build relationships with discharge planners, home health and hospice agencies, elder law attorneys, churches and physicians, and host useful local events like a caregiver support group.

What should an assisted living marketing plan include?

A short list of activities you will do every month, who does each one, and how you will measure them. A simple 90-day plan: fix the basics in month 1, start a referral visit rhythm and review requests in month 2, and add one paid test in month 3 if rooms are still open.

How do we measure assisted living marketing?

Count inquiries, tours and move-ins by source every month, and work out cost per move-in for each source. Ask every family how they heard about you and write it down. Look at several months together, because move-ins come slowly in a small home.

How do senior living referral services get paid?

They commonly charge the community a fee when a family they referred moves in, often tied to roughly the first month's rent and care. Terms vary by contract, so read your own agreement, including how a referral is defined and how long it counts.

Can we target older adults with Google or Facebook ads?

Assume not. Google's rules for housing ads in the US and Canada do not allow targeting by age, gender, parental status, marital status or ZIP code, and senior living advertisers should assume these rules apply to them. Meta has a Housing special ad category with similar limits, so check before you build an audience.
